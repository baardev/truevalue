Turning the Tide: The ‘3Cs’ of Blue Finance and the Transitional Need for Blended Finance

  • Report this article
Blue Economy Pulse

Blue Economy Pulse

Monitoring the pulse of Blue Economy activity around the world.

Published Apr 23, 2026
+ Follow

By Ben Tovell, NLAI Consulting Associate

Following the United Nations Ocean Conference, held in Nice in 2025, there has been much hope for the potential of blue finance and its increasing role in mobilizing capital to protect and restore ocean and coastal ecosystems, whilst supporting sustainable economic activity for coastal communities. However, many barriers remain that are holding back Blue Economy investment deployment at scale. Fast-forward nine months and the IMF-World Bank meetings (held in Washington, DC, between April 13 – 18, 2026) highlighted the role of blended finance as a tool to support international development and ways it might leverage private investment.

Blended finance is especially relevant in seeking to support Blue Economy development in the Global South, via blue finance mechanisms. ‘Blue finance’ refers to the set of financial instruments, policies, and investment strategies that support the sustainable use, conservation, and restoration of ocean and coastal ecosystems. This three-part Blue Finance Series will discuss the how and the why of blended finance, as it seeks to support Blue Economy development in coastal communities. However, before doing so, we discuss fundamental enablers for blue finance from both bottom-up and top-down perspectives.

The ‘3Cs’ and Blue Finance

 Before a rational investor or lender considers an investment decision, they will firstly look for the following characteristics in the potential proponent: Character, Capacity and Collateral. In general, it is on the basis of these three bottom-up principles that finance is given (or withheld). The 4th ‘C’ is investment Consistency, which leads to investment Certainty: this is a top-down approach. Both seek to create an investible or bankable transaction.

Character First

Character is by far the most important of these principles. It refers to the ability and willingness to perform and repay funding. It is essentially a judgment call that will be made by someone who has only just met you. Character is tested by your credit history, deal execution history, how well prepared you are for your discussions with investors, and how clearly you can answer their questions, amongst other factors. If an investor has any doubt at all about your character, it is more likely that talks will not proceed any further.

Capacity Next

Capacity refers to the technical feasibility or economic viability (of a project) to generate a return on the money for its investors. Identifying the underlying financing need (e.g., loan tenor, amount, type of investment, etc.), before approaching investors will also ensure meetings are more targeted. If capacity is executed well, then the way in which the investors view character should also improve.

Collateral Last

Collateral is last, because all good investors make investment decisions on the basis that the expected source of repayment is sufficiently strong to provide a return under a range of scenarios. In other words, they have assessed the character and the capacity (of a project) as being of sufficient quality to support a reasonable expectation that a return will be provided. Collateral is likely not enough of a reason to unilaterally make an investment decision without the other two aspects being first assessed as adequate.

Application to Blue Finance (or Ocean Investment)

Blue finance focuses on activities that seek to address the intertwined triple planetary pressures of climate change, biodiversity loss, and pollution, for it is the ocean where these three forces converge most visibly and dangerously for the ecosystems, food security, economies, and human lives and livelihoods that depend upon it.

Blue finance is grounded in the recognition that healthy oceans underpin global climate regulation, food security, and economic stability, and therefore requires dedicated, well‑structured financial flows to maintain and restore their ecological integrity.

Maturity and perceived risk of Blue Finance

As discussed in a former Blue Economy Pulse article, The Psychology of Ocean Investment – Is Confirmation Bias Holding Us Back?, blue finance is often framed as ‘emerging’ for a number of reasons, with investors tending to gravitate towards that which they most understand or are familiar with. Uncertainty may be related to a psychological phenomenon called ‘confirmation bias’. In investment terms, this relates to the 3Cs and investment ‘certainty’ – or at a minimum, consistency. These bottom-up and top-down approaches and market maturity will be explored throughout this three-part Blue Finance series.

CHARACTER

A track record that attracts sustained investment in the Blue Economy is currently lacking, which contributes to a lack of investible pipelines. Capacity-building (referred to below) is crucial in seeking to build the blocks that can generate capacity, and, in turn, track record.

Providing additional support is blended finance, which seeks to de-risk some blue investments, by improving the risk–return profile of untested projects, business models or revenue streams that would otherwise be too risky – or too early‑stage – for commercial investors. Blended finance (to be discussed in detail in Part 3 of this Blue Finance Series) is a transitional architecture that is currently necessary to support capacity, track record, and ultimately, character.

CAPACITY to support Blue Finance

The ability to meet the return expectations of investors in the Blue Economy depends on the ability of a project proponent to generate a return for investors. There are emerging ways in which costs can be flexed down (somewhat controllable) and revenues increased (less controllable but open to a number of emerging opportunities).

Flexing costs down through technology and standardization

Ways in which Blue Economy project costs can be flexed down over time might include:

  • Standardized terms & conditions in investment term sheets & full investment/lending documentation, standardized investment memorandums & investment decks, and standardized credit & project diligence processes;
  • Digitalization of the above standardized documentation & diligence items to further reduce project costs;
  • The creation of digital investment platforms suitable to both project proponents and investors to further ease standardized investment access, with investors able to readily compare investments in the same formats, whilst also allowing potential project aggregation, including at a regional level; and
  • Integrating the use of AI and other technology solutions to support human interventions (e.g., measuring or monitoring above and below ground biomass to calculate carbon stock changes in mangroves), whilst maintaining credible and transparent alignment to globally accepted frameworks & methodologies.

Emerging avenues for revenue creation 

As the ‘true’ value of ecosystem services derived from nature emerges, opportunities also emerge for new revenue sources. The benefits that nature provides to people include regulating climate, supporting food systems, protecting coasts, and cultural and recreational value. These novel revenue sources may emanate from the same intervention or the same project area, where revenues were previously absent.

Landscape Approach and Symbiotic Income Streams

Blue carbon credit transactions may be considered from a ‘landscape approach’ (i.e., not just one habitat type or area). Mangroves retreat onto land but also grow in the intertidal zone. They are often intertwined with seagrass and coral reefs in one ecosystem. As such, capturing multiple habitats in an intervention should be a core project aim, wherever possible, especially as, in reality, these are connected socially, culturally and economically.

Likewise, the symbiotic income streams derived from a mangroves project that concurrently boosts nursery fish through habitat provision, or through interlinked revenues derived from a self-sustaining and executable Marine Protected Area (MPA) (e.g., eco-tourism, fishing and diving fees), are examples of this approach.

Blue Natural Capital & Resilience

Ongoing developments in measuring natural capital co-benefits of a given blue carbon transaction (e.g., property flood protection, reduced pollution, enhanced fisheries and increased tourism), through initiatives like the Global Ocean Accounting Partnership, provide potential future income sources from revenue streams in the form of externalities that have not traditionally been measured (and therefore protected). This is because you cannot effectively manage what you cannot measure; you also cannot invest in what you cannot price.

Quantifying the adaptation and resilience value of the same mangrove interventions or other Nature-based Solution (NbS) to include avoided property losses, reduced capital expenditures (CAPEX) and operational expenditures (OPEX), and, in the longer-term, reduced climate risk, supports the argument that nature-based investments can compete on equal terms with “grey” solutions in investment decisions and national planning. This is likely to lead to long-term climate adaptation and resilience benefits for government and the private sector. Indeed, when NbS are associated with large-scale infrastructure systems such as railways, roads and energy corridors – they can lower maintenance costs and improve long-term asset performance, as well as reduce climate risks over time.

In the future, the value of natural capital benefits will likely receive greater acknowledgment, which would be positive for ocean investing, especially for those blue carbon projects currently deemed to be uneconomic. From a government perspective, the ‘landscape approach’ might benefit from integrating blue carbon ecosystems into national climate strategies through Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) and National Adaptation Plans (NAPs).

Building Capacity and Character

Many of the emerging revenue sources above rely on enabling conditions to ‘catch up’. For example, they rely on consistent global frameworks and standards, which also align with jurisdictional government ocean planning, legislation and policy, supported by sound governance (This will be covered in Part 2 of this Blue Finance series.)

The monetization of these emerging revenue sources also relies on credible and transparent science that is translated into the language of finance; and, crucially, they rely on partnerships that facilitate investment activities in the ocean economy.

Beyond this, the following are examples of ways to approach building capacity, and in turn character, in and around coastal communities and throughout different levels of government:

Financial Literacy

Building the financial capabilities required for a community, enterprise, or local institution to participate effectively in a blue finance mechanism is key. This might include: 

  • Education on livelihood diversification opportunities that seek to reduce harmful activities, such as overfishing or harvesting mangroves for fuel. Examples are: payment for ecosystem services models, blue carbon finance, sustainable aquaculture, sustainable fisheries, sustainable forestry products, and other alternative sustainable livelihood models that can provide economic alternatives to support coastal communities.
  • Education on relevant blue finance instruments: e.g., blue carbon credits, microfinance, blue bonds, blended finance, or other blue finance types or models (described above).
  • Capacity-building to help coastal communities meet eligibility requirements for carbon credit methodologies or to help governments source and execute projects that sit inside Blue Bond frameworks. Recent notable examples include the Blue Carbon Academy as it relates to blue carbon, and The Commonwealth’s Blue Bond guidance, as it relates to building projects pipelines that can be financed under a Blue Bond.
  • Training on financial statements and financial analysis: i.e., profit and loss, cashflow and balance sheet statements, as well as budgeting, generating forecasts and managing stable cashflows, tailored for local communities, and suitable for financiers.
  • Support in preparing and translating any of the above into investor-compliant presentations, pitch decks, and investment or lending documentation, as well as integration into digital format, will also help market access (mentioned below).
  • Targeted knowledge-transfer on currency hedging (since many projects are denominated in hard currencies, such as USD) and guarantees for government stakeholders and project proponents.

Success will more likely be achieved by providing examples of sustainable ocean governance that manages marine spaces in a transparent, science‑driven, and inclusive way to ensure healthy ecosystems, fair community benefits, and clear rules and responsibilities for resource use that protect both ocean health and long‑term economic resilience. Community and investor confidence could also be fostered by providing examples of what projects have – or haven’t – worked in the past (regionally or further afield).

Collateral Types for Marine, Aquaculture, and Nature-Based Finance

Likewise, some emerging blue finance structures may provide a combination of the above collateral types and structures. Such nature-linked collateral might include cross-guarantees from project operators to kick-off projects and (symbiotic) fisheries revenues that are supported by a mangroves project intervention, which may both be secured by investors or lenders.

As noted above, whilst collateral supports investment decisions, it is the capacity and character of the project proponent that matters most.

Market access is what matters to support scaling of blue finance

Local coastal or indigenous communities (LCs) are integral to blue finance transactions since every credible ocean investment ultimately depends on the long-term wellbeing, rights, and economic resilience of the people who live with – and from – the sea. Indeed, it is the LCs, as stewards of coastal ecosystems, whose actions determine whether ecological gains endure or face project reversal. Therefore, many blue finance projects must be designed with sustainable livelihoods for LCs as a core design component. From an investment perspective, success would be more likely with local stewardship and community buy-in.

Furthermore, since they are stewards of the coastal communities, LCs already know how to fish, and they know how to collect and recycle plastic. What many coastal communities often don’t have is market access: the ability to get their products to market. Therefore, in terms of building capacity and character, strengthening early-stage pipelines in coastal communities through facilitating regional partnerships is a crucial enabler of ocean investments, facilitated by coastal communities.

Overall, capacity-building that moves the dial from commitment to delivery should focus on translating local knowledge into credible investible ocean models, combined with supporting coordinated market access. Community-centred design is a commonsense investment derisking mechanism, a growing compliance requirement, and a moral imperative for activating the sustainable Blue Economy.
