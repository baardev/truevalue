Executive Summary

Is It Possible to Estimate? Yes — With Important Caveats.

A common instinct when facing a question like this is to conclude that there are simply too many variables to provide a meaningful estimate. That instinct is wrong. There are many variables — but they are knowable, classifiable, and bounded. The cost of deploying the TrueValue Framework into a commodity supply chain is estimable in the same way any large institutional project is estimable: by decomposing the work into phases, mapping the required labour and infrastructure at each phase, and stress-testing the assumptions against analogous frameworks that have already been built.

The key finding of this analysis is that the TrueValue Framework is deployable at a cost that is modest relative to the value it unlocks — and well within the range of comparable certification, transparency, and impact-measurement systems that the industry has already funded.

The critical cost driver is not technology — it is data opacity. Section 1

The Stakeholder Ecosystem: Who Is Involved and What They Cost

Deploying the TrueValue Framework is not a technology project. It is a coordination project — building agreement across parties who have historically benefited from the information asymmetries the Framework is designed to measure and reduce. Understanding the stakeholder landscape is the prerequisite to any credible cost estimate.

Each stakeholder type incurs a different category of cost: some require legal negotiation (data sharing, IP, audit rights); some require technical integration (API access, custody system hooks); some require change management (training, process change, incentive alignment); and some require regulatory engagement (standards bodies, financial regulators, government ministries).

Steady-State Annual Operating Cost (Post Year 5)

Once fully deployed, the Framework transitions from a build cost to an operating cost. Steady-state annual operations for a full gold supply chain implementation are estimated at $3.0M–$6.0M/year, covering: a core team of 10–15 FTE, ongoing audit cycles, platform maintenance, and annual recertification of participating entities. This cost is recoverable through a framework licensing or participation fee structure, making long-run self-funding achievable at scale.

Key Variables: What Could Materially Change the Estimate

Factors That Reduce Cost & Timeline

  • Regulatory mandate: If LBMA or WGC require TrueValue participation as a condition of accreditation, data access costs fall 40–60% and timeline compresses by 12–18 months
  • Major mining co-sponsors: If 2–3 major miners (e.g. Newmont, Barrick) co-fund as pilot participants, Phase 2 cost drops by $1–2M and credibility is established early
  • Shared platform with Shea: Parallel deployment saves 20–25% on technology and management overhead
  • Development finance grants (Shea): IFC, GIZ, and EU grants could cover 30–50% of Shea deployment costs; Shea becomes near-self-funding
  • SaaS technology approach: Using existing supply chain data platforms (e.g. Sourcemap, GreenToken) rather than bespoke build saves $1–2M in Phase 0–1
  • Academic partnerships: CSIRO, LBMA research arm, university partnerships reduce methodology validation cost by $200–500k

Factors That Increase Cost & Timeline

  • Refiner/logistics resistance: If LBMA-accredited refineries or vault operators refuse data sharing, legal contestation adds $500k–$2M and 12–24 months
  • Regulatory inaction: Without mandate, voluntary adoption in the gold midstream may take 3–5 extra years and $3–5M in additional stakeholder engagement
  • ASM (artisanal mining) inclusion: Including artisanal and small-scale mining in Phase 1 adds complexity, field cost, and 6–12 months; may require separate sub-framework
  • Geopolitical disruption: Operations in jurisdictions with political instability (DRC, Mali, Sudan) add security cost, legal risk, and data quality challenges
  • ESG litigation risk: Opaque phase operators may challenge Framework disclosures legally; contingency of $500k–$2M should be reserved
  • Currency & inflation: Multi-currency operations in 15+ countries; 10–15% cost buffer recommended for exchange rate and local inflation risk

The Critical Path

The deployment is not constrained by technology or money — it is constrained by data access at three gold supply chain phases: Doré/Intermediate (Phase 3), Refining (Phase 4), and Logistics & Vaulting (Phase 6). These phases are architecturally designed for opacity. Obtaining reliable, auditable data requires either regulatory mandate (fastest path) or extended voluntary negotiation with individual operators (slowest, most expensive path). Every other workstream can run in parallel; these three data negotiations are serial dependencies.

The recommended first action is therefore not a technology build — it is a regulatory engagement strategy: approaching LBMA and WGC with a co-development proposal that frames TrueValue as an extension of their existing responsible sourcing infrastructure, not a competitive alternative. This positions the Framework to benefit from mandatory data flows rather than having to negotiate each one individually.

Section 7

The Investment Case: Cost vs. Value Unlocked

Implementation costs must be evaluated against the structural value the Framework is designed to unlock. The Investor Report documents a 71% cumulative value advantage for TrueValue-managed chains over a 10-year horizon. Even at conservative estimates of the gold supply chain's annual value (~$250B), the Framework's optimisation potential — capturing just 1% of currently leaked structural value — generates annual returns of $2.5B+. The implementation cost of $13–$30M is a rounding error relative to that potential.

Who Should Fund This — and Why

  • Major mining companies (co-development): benefit from ESG premium pricing and reduced regulatory risk. Contribution: $1–3M each for 3–5 anchor participants
  • Institutional investors (impact/ESG mandates): obtain phase-resolved supply chain risk data not available elsewhere. Contribution: $2–5M total as consortium
  • LBMA / WGC (standards body mandate): natural home for ongoing operations; existing regulatory infrastructure reduces deployment friction by 40–60%
  • Development finance (Shea): IFC, GIZ, EU grants could cover 30–50% of Shea deployment; Shea becomes proof-of-concept for gold
  • ESG data platforms (Bloomberg, MSCI, Sustainalytics): TrueValue scores are a premium data product; licensing revenue can self-fund steady-state operations within 3–4 years

What Happens If It Is Not Built

  • Continued structural opacity at Phases 3, 4, and 6 means investors cannot price hidden constraint accumulation — the exact risk profile that generates sudden, correlated supply chain failures
  • ESG capital reallocation pressure increases without structural data to defend supply chain choices; gold and commodity producers face growing cost-of-capital premium with no measurement tool to respond
  • Regulatory gap: EU CSRD, SEC climate disclosure, and OECD due diligence requirements are moving toward exactly the kind of phase-level supply chain data TrueValue provides; first movers capture the compliance alignment premium
  • Shea value leakage continues: $25–80M/year in unmonetised water and biodiversity assets remains off the balance sheet; women farmers receive below-market compensation with no structural measurement to demonstrate or negotiate against

The Implementation Thesis in One Sentence

At $13–$30M over five years for gold, and $1.8–$4M over three years for shea, the TrueValue Framework is not an expensive project — it is a modestly priced structural measurement system for commodity chains that collectively move trillions of dollars of value annually, and whose hidden imbalances represent multiples of the Framework's cost in annually leaking value.

Appendix

Methodology & Estimation Assumptions

Cost Estimation Methodology

All cost estimates use a bottom-up labour-hours approach: role types are identified per phase, staffing levels estimated based on analogous project deployments, and blended day rates applied by role (analyst: $800–$1,400/day; engineer: $900–$1,500/day; field researcher: $400–$700/day; legal: $300–$600/hr). Ranges reflect the difference between a cooperative industry posture (low) and a resistant one (high).

Analogous Framework Sources

  • LBMA Responsible Gold Guidance — public annual reports and industry cost surveys
  • Fairtrade International — annual report financial statements; academic analysis of standard-setting costs
  • CDP — published annual reports and 10-year retrospectives
  • GRI — organisational history documentation and budget disclosures
  • Acorn Carbon Programme — Serious Shea / Rabobank programme documentation
  • Foran, Lenzen & Dey (2005) — TBL methodology precedent; CSIRO operational research

Key Assumptions

  • Day rates are in 2026 USD; no forward inflation adjustment applied
  • Technology platform assumes 60% SaaS / 40% custom build hybrid
  • Contingency of 15% applied to all phases
  • Gold pilot covers 2–3 major mine operations, 1 LBMA refinery, and COMEX exchange data
  • Shea pilot covers Acorn-enrolled cooperatives in Burkina Faso and Senegal
  • Steady-state cost assumes industry cost-sharing via licensing fees from Year 4 onwards

What This Estimate Does Not Cover

  • Policy lobbying costs (if regulatory mandate route pursued)
  • Marketing and external communications for Framework adoption
  • Third-party academic peer review and journal publication
  • Translation and localisation costs for non-English jurisdictions
  • Litigation costs above the 15% contingency reserve
  • Costs borne by participating entities to integrate with the Framework (estimated separately at $200k–$1M per major participant)
