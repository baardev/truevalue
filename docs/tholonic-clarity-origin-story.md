# The Long Road to the Triangle: How the Tholonic Model Found the Clarity Coalition

*A story of fifty years, a few strange coincidences, and a discovery hiding inside the oldest question anyone ever asked.*

---

## Part One: The Question That Would Not Sit Still

It started, as these things usually do, with a young man reading too widely and noticing something he could not explain away.

The year was somewhere in the early 1970s. Jeff was not yet any particular kind of professional. He was the kind of person who followed a thread wherever it went, and the thread he had picked up led, in short order, from comparative religion into philosophy into the history of science and then sideways into corners of the library that did not get much traffic: alchemical texts, Neoplatonic treatises, Eastern philosophy, the dense metaphysical systems of the Renaissance. He was not looking for any of this. He was looking at it because each thing he read pointed toward the next thing, and the next thing kept pointing back at the same shape.

The shape was this: too many very different people, separated by thousands of years and thousands of miles and entire civilizations of incompatible belief, had built their models of reality the same way.

Not superficially. Not the way all myths have a hero. The Sumerians, the Greeks, the Taoists, the medieval alchemists, the Neoplatonists in Alexandria, the Renaissance metaphysicians in Florence: the architecture of their arguments kept coming out the same way. There was always a source. The source differentiated into two distinct principles, forces, or elements. And from the interaction of those two, a third thing emerged: the stable state, the reconciled form, the thing that held.

Three. Everywhere, three.

Pythagoras had built his entire philosophy around triangular number relationships. Hegel's thesis-antithesis-synthesis was only the most recent Western label for a template that appeared independently in traditions that had no contact with each other. The Tao Te Ching opened with the observation that the Tao produced one, one produced two, two produced three, and three produced the ten thousand things. The alchemists had sulfur, mercury, and salt. The Christian Trinity was only the most culturally prominent instance. What Jeff could see, and what nobody in any of the academic fields he was reading appeared to be asking, was whether these resemblances pointed at something structural and real underneath all the cultural noise.

There was no field for this question. No journal. No body of literature working the seam between comparative religion, mathematics, and the history of science with this specific focus. The historians of religion had documented the patterns comparatively but had not tried to formalize them. The philosophers of science had not looked backward to ask whether the scientific method was itself another instance of the same template. The mathematicians were not reading mythology. The mythologists were not reading mathematics. Jeff was alone with the problem in the way that matters: no faculty to consult, no existing dissertation to argue with, no established scholar who had walked this particular path and could tell him whether it led somewhere or nowhere.

He decided it led somewhere. He kept walking.

Over the following decade the research ranged from laboratories to monasteries, sometimes in the same week. Jeff was not the kind of person who stayed on one side of the wall between the scientific and the contemplative. He sat in on physics lectures and Zen retreats. He read primary alchemical texts alongside thermodynamics papers. He interviewed working scientists and visited communities of practice that had been thinking about the structure of reality in unbroken lineages for centuries. Each encounter added a data point. None of them resolved the question. What they did, collectively, was make the pattern harder to dismiss. It appeared in too many independent contexts, developed by too many people with no knowledge of each other, to be coincidence. The structure was either something real about reality or it was something very deep in the way human minds model the world, and Jeff was not yet sure those two explanations were actually different.

In the late 1970s he began writing code. The first home computers had arrived, and Jeff was there early, working through the emerging discipline of software architecture at the same time he was reading philosophy. Programming turned out to be useful for the investigation in a way he had not expected. Writing code forces a kind of precision that discourages vague analogies. If a pattern is real, you should be able to specify it. If you can specify it, you should be able to implement it. If you can implement it, you can test it. The discipline of software gave the philosophical investigation a new standard of rigor to aspire to. He had the pattern. He did not yet have the specification.

Then came Polaroid.

Jeff was working in optical physics, and the collaboration brought him into contact with some of the leading research scientists working on the physical behavior of light. Optics is a domain where the mathematics of wave behavior, refraction, polarization, and interference had been worked out with great precision over centuries. What Jeff found in that domain was the same thing he had been finding everywhere else: the same three-role structure underlying the physical description of how light moved and changed. A wave constrained by a medium. An output modified by the geometry of its passage. The stable state that emerged from the negotiation between the two. The specific people he was working with were rigorous experimentalists who had no patience for loose analogies, and that sharpened his thinking considerably. He was being forced, field by field, to articulate the pattern with more precision and less metaphor.

Each professional chapter added something. Optical physics gave him a deep intuition about wave dynamics and the behavior of constrained physical systems. Programming gave him the habit of specification. The philosophical investigation gave him the comparative depth. The monastic research gave him access to several thousand years of careful thinking about the nature of reality that mainstream academia had mostly stopped reading. Together they were building something, but what they were building was still, at this point, little more than a confederation of somewhat connected ideas. He could see the resemblances. He could argue the case comparatively. He could not yet derive the whole thing from first principles and show you why it had to be this way.

That part was still coming.

One observation that had grown more solid through all of it: the scientific method itself, the framework that had superseded every one of the older models Jeff was studying, carried the same shape. A hypothesis defines and constrains. An experiment contributes evidence. The negotiation between the two produces a result. Even the most rigorous and disenchanted tool for making knowledge about the world was built from three roles, and the roles were the same ones. The pattern was not a relic of pre-scientific thinking. It was the scaffold that had survived the transition from theology to physics without anyone noticing, because everyone was focused on what had changed and no one was looking at what had stayed exactly the same.

## Part Two: The Architect Meets the Web

By the mid-1990s Jeff was working as an IT architect and researcher at IBM, and the timing put him at the edge of something he had not anticipated.

The internet was not new. What was new was the World Wide Web, and what was being argued about at the frontier of web development was not just a technical question but a philosophical one. A document web stored and linked text. You could find things. But the web could not understand anything, could not reason about relationships, could not tell you that the gold produced at a mine in Nevada was related to the gold registered at a vault in London by a chain of custody that passed through four jurisdictions and three refining stages. It could store those facts. It could not know what they meant.

Tim Berners-Lee, who had invented the web in the first place and therefore had some standing to say what it should become next, was proposing something he called the semantic web. The unit of semantic information would not be the document. It would be the statement: a structured assertion of the form subject, predicate, object. "The mine is in Nevada." "The refinery transforms ore into bullion." "The vault holds registered bars." Each statement linked not just to other statements but carried, within its own structure, the relationship between the things it described. Three elements, arranged into a claim. You could not split a statement into anything smaller and still have meaning.

The word for this three-element unit was triplet.

Jeff was working at the edge of this development, thinking through the architectural implications, when the recognition arrived. It was not dramatic. It was more like a geometric proof snapping into focus, the moment when you see that two triangles are congruent and cannot unsee it.

The triplet was the same thing he had been looking at since the early 1970s.

Subject, predicate, object. Bounding, integrating, instantiating. Definition, Contribution, Negotiation. The source, the differentiation, the emergent stable state. Every formulation he had spent decades tracking was a different cultural or disciplinary notation for the same three-part structure, and here it was again, sitting at the foundation of the architecture of the largest information system ever built by human beings.

This also connected directly to something that mathematicians and logicians had been independently arguing: that the triad is the most irreducible non-trivial structure that can exist. A formal statement of this would eventually appear decades later in what became Lemma 4.2 of the third paper in the Tholonic series, which demonstrated rigorously that two variables cannot simultaneously carry functionally distinct bounding and integrating roles, and that a system with a distinguished state and two functionally independent auxiliaries cannot be reduced below three variables without collapsing into either stasis or divergence. The irreducibility of the triad was not a mystical claim. It was a theorem.

Jeff could not yet prove it in that form. But he could feel the ground underneath it.

## Part Three: The Long Derivation

There is a quality to mathematical work that is hard to describe to someone who has not done it: the long period in which you know approximately what you are looking for but cannot find the language that will make it real. You have the intuition. You have the cross-domain evidence. You have the shape of the thing. What you do not have is the formal derivation, the chain of steps that would let another person follow you from premises to conclusion without having to take anything on trust.

Jeff spent years in that condition.

The question was how to ground the pattern mathematically. The answer he eventually found was to start not with the full complexity of the triadic structure but with the simplest object he could identify that still had generative power.

He started with 0 and 2.

Zero is not a number in the ordinary sense. It is the concept of distinction itself: the existence of a boundary between "something" and "nothing," between the state and its absence. It is the precondition for any counting or any difference. Two is the smallest number that is both prime and generative: the minimal unit of difference, the first moment where one thing can be distinguished from another. Zero and two together are the most stripped-down version of what is required to begin.

From 0 and 2, following the structural patterns he had been tracing, the mathematics resolved forward into the first three prime numbers: 2, 3, and 5. This was not an arbitrary stopping point. These three are the only primes that correspond to distinct structural identities: duality, triplicity, and the first compound structure that cannot be produced from either of the first two alone. Assign each to one of the three roles in the triadic framework. Apply the recursive structure where each level contains the pattern of the whole and is itself contained by the next level up.

Then see what comes out.

What came out was not what Jeff had expected, though afterward he could not say he was entirely surprised.

All five of the fundamental mathematical constants appeared.

Not as inputs. As outputs. As derivable consequences of the recursive triadic logic applied to the first three primes. Pi. Phi, the golden ratio. The square root of two. The natural logarithm of two. Euler's number, e. The five constants that appear throughout physics, geometry, information theory, biology, and finance as the irreducible scaffolding of quantitative reality. They had always been treated as separate discoveries, each one found in a different context by different people at different times. What Jeff found was that they were not separate. They were a single structure. All five of them emerged together, from one derivation, because they were all expressions of the same underlying recursive relationship.

This was the moment the model got its name.

The structure was holarchical: each level was simultaneously a whole and a part of the next level, in the sense that Arthur Koestler had described with the word "holon." The geometry was tetrahedral. A tetrahedron has four vertices, and any three vertices form a triangle, and each triangle encodes the three-way relationship at one level of the hierarchy. The structure was recursive, repeating without alteration through every scale. Tholonia: from "holon," with the tetrahedral geometry folded into the name. A single unit of this structure was a tholon. The model of how tholons nested and interacted and produced stable systems was the Tholonic Model.

Fifty years after the question had first lodged itself in his mind, Jeff had an answer that was mathematical, derivable, auditable, and rooted in the most irreducible objects in mathematics. The pattern he had been tracking through Sumerian cosmology and Greek geometry and alchemical symbolism and Leibnizian notation had a formal proof underneath it. It had been there the whole time.

## Part Four: A Marine Biologist Decides the World Needs Fixing

Dr. Sarah Jones arrived at the Clarity Coalition from a different direction, by way of marine biology, a doctorate, and a very clear-eyed assessment of what the world's sustainability frameworks were actually doing.

The doctorate had given her something useful: a biologist's habit of looking at systems the way they actually work rather than the way they are supposed to work. Ocean ecosystems do not care about institutional mandates or reporting frameworks. They respond to real flows: nutrient cycles, temperature gradients, predator-prey ratios. A biologist who wanted to understand a fishery had to understand the chain of dependencies that sustained it, from the phytoplankton at the bottom to the commercial vessels at the top. You could not skip steps. You could not substitute a narrative for a mechanism.

She brought this habit to sustainability work and found, consistently, that the frameworks in use were not built the way a biologist would build them. They described outcomes rather than structures. They measured what came out of a system rather than what was happening inside it. Carbon emissions, biodiversity indices, welfare indicators: all valuable, all real, all downstream of the actual physical and economic processes that produced them. If you wanted to change the outcomes, you had to reach the chains. And most of the frameworks she encountered were architecturally incapable of seeing the chains.

She had the scientific training, the network of relationships, and the specific combination of patience and impatience that serious institution-building requires. So she built one. The Clarity Coalition was launched in London in September 2014, with the explicit purpose of bridging the gap between investment flows and global resource realities, working from the bottom up, combining local knowledge with scalable analytical frameworks, and taking the long view on what a transition to genuinely sustainable production would actually require. The name was deliberate. What the existing landscape lacked was not data or intentions. It lacked clarity about what was actually happening in the systems that mattered.

The data she and her colleagues built up over the following years was structured around supply chains, because supply chains were the mechanism. A commodity supply chain is, at its most basic level, a sequence of phases. Each phase takes something in, transforms it, and passes something out. Each phase operates under specific constraints that define what it can do and what it cannot. Each phase contributes specific outputs that feed the next phase. The entire chain is a nested set of input-output relationships, each one constrained by the physics and economics and governance of that particular stage.

She did not know yet that this structure had a mathematical description that had been derived independently by a former IBM researcher working through a fifty-year investigation of the deep structure of reality. That conversation was coming, though neither of them knew it.

## Part Five: The Conversation

The meeting did not happen at a conference where they were both presenting. It did not happen because someone made an introduction over email. The specific circumstances were the kind that, afterward, are hard to explain without resorting to the word luck, which is accurate but not entirely satisfying.

What can be said is that when the conversation started, it did not take long.

Sarah's supply chain data mapped onto the Tholonic framework with a precision that was, as Jeff put it later, unsettling in the best possible way. The phases she had been documenting had Definition: the constraints, limits, and requirements that bounded what each phase could do. They had Contribution: the outputs, flows, and handoffs that connected one phase to the next. And the relationship between those two things, the balance or imbalance between what a phase required and what it produced, was exactly what the model predicted would determine whether a node was coherent or under stress.

The triplets that had opened a conceptual door during the IBM years were now appearing as the natural unit of supply chain analysis. Not as an imposed structure. As the structure that was already there, waiting to be named.

The fit was not approximate. Both of them had been working, from entirely different starting points, toward a point where the structure of the one matched the structure of the other. The mathematician who had spent decades deriving a formal framework for how systems sustain themselves had met the scientist who had spent years collecting data from real systems trying to sustain themselves, and the framework fit the data because the framework described what the data was about.

## Part Six: What Was Found

The mathematical result was cleaner than either of them had anticipated.

The five constants were not just abstract outputs of the derivation. Each one measured a specific structural dimension of any real system. Pi measured operational balance across the chain as a whole. Phi measured whether value was being distributed in the proportions that allowed the whole chain to remain functional. The square root of two measured structural overhead: the degree to which constraint was consuming energy without producing output. The natural logarithm of two measured transformation efficiency across the time lags, capital gaps, and biological delays that every real supply chain contained. Euler's number measured financial abstraction: how tightly the funding instruments were connected to real operational performance rather than floating above it.

Measure a supply chain along all five dimensions. Benchmark each score against the corresponding constant. What you get is a phase-resolved health map that locates stress before the stress becomes visible in the outputs. A system can be losing coherence internally for months or years before the failure shows up in the numbers that anyone is watching. The model does not wait for the failure. It reads the structure.

This was something new. Not a better scorecard. A different kind of instrument: one that looked at the shape of what was happening rather than the level of what was coming out, and that could tell the difference between a system constrained by its structure and a system constrained by its efficiency, because those two problems look identical in aggregate output figures but require entirely different remedies.

For Sarah's work, the consequences were immediate and practical. Supply chains had always been analyzable in terms of flows. Now they were analyzable in terms of structure, and that structure could be compared across chains, sectors, and scales using a common mathematical language that did not depend on the specific commodity or the specific geography. The same five dimensions that described the health of a gold refinery in Switzerland described the health of a shea butter supply chain in Burkina Faso. The mathematics did not care what was flowing through the chain. It cared about the shape of the flow.

## Part Seven: How Far the Triangle Reaches

The first serious test of any analytical framework is not whether it works on the problem that generated it. It is whether it works on problems it was not designed for.

The Tholonic Model has now been applied across a range of domains wide enough to test that question seriously, and the list makes more sense as a list than it would as a narrative, because the point is the breadth.

**Physical sciences.** The solar system's orbital mechanics, analyzed through Newton's own papers and his less-known alchemical writings, score coherently against the five tholonic constants. The atom has been mapped as a measurable tholon, with the nucleus, electron cloud, and the electromagnetic field playing the three structural roles and producing quantitative predictions about atomic behavior. The model's structure has been compared formally against twistor geometry, a framework in mathematical physics, and found to share deep structural parallels. Photosynthesis, as a transformation process with defined inputs and outputs and a bounded conversion efficiency, has been analyzed as a supply chain in the Tholonic sense. The Standard Model of particle physics has been examined as a candidate for replacement or extension by the Tholonic framework, on the grounds that the Standard Model's parameter count may be reducible through the triadic structure.

**Medicine and biology.** Cancer has been analyzed as a progressive D-collapse: each driver mutation disables a constraint, lowering the balance score that determines whether a cell maintains its coherent state, until at metastasis the constraint apparatus dissolves entirely and the proliferative drive runs unchecked. The model produces quantitative predictions about tumor malignancy grades that map onto existing WHO classification thresholds. Cellular aging has been analyzed as a related process. Consciousness itself has been mapped: the Default Mode Network functions as the D-apparatus, sensory prediction errors as the C-apparatus, and the stable sense of self as the emergent N-state. From this mapping, the therapeutic window for psychedelic-assisted therapy becomes a specific B-score band, and the mechanism of ego dissolution becomes a precise tholonic prediction rather than a phenomenological description.

**Commodity supply chains.** This is the domain where the Tholonic Model and the Clarity Coalition have done the most sustained empirical work together. Gold, from geological formation to COMEX-registered bullion across eight phases. West African shea, from village-level harvest through international commodity markets. Cocoa in the Netherlands and at the international level. Olive oil in southern Spain, specifically the Marina Alta and Andalucia regions. Ammonia and fertilizer chains. Lithium battery supply chains. Solar photovoltaic manufacturing. The global electricity grid. In each case the same five-dimensional analysis produces a phase-resolved health map that locates structural stress before it becomes visible in outputs.

**Water and infrastructure.** The Jackson, Mississippi municipal water crisis was analyzed retrospectively as a structural failure locatable in specific phases of a water system supply chain. New Water and the Orange County Water District have been analyzed as forward-looking cases of water recycling infrastructure under structural stress and transition.

**Ecosystems and land use.** The Danube basin has been analyzed as a multi-stakeholder ecosystem with both natural and human supply chains interacting across the same basin. Blue carbon systems. The Gran Chaco in South America. Senegal agroforestry. Bristol's One City sustainability initiative. The Great Green Wall across the Sahel, where the Clarity Coalition has done field-level work, is a candidate for the most ambitious sustained application: an 8,000-kilometer living infrastructure project whose structural health can be tracked phase by phase as the wall develops.

**Finance and economic history.** The Lehman Brothers collapse has been analyzed as a structural failure in which the D-apparatus of the financial system (the constraint and oversight structures) degraded to the point where C (the proliferative financial activity) had no effective bounding, producing a system-level N-collapse that anyone alive in 2008 watched happen in real time. The Silicon Valley Bank failure was analyzed using the same framework. Economic history as a whole has been treated as a sequence of large-scale tholonic systems, each with phases that can be scored and transitions that can be located structurally rather than only explained narratively after the fact.

**Artificial intelligence.** This one deserves its own section, and it follows below.

## Part Eight: The New Door

The story might have ended there, as the account of a framework and a dataset finding each other and becoming, together, more useful than either was alone. But it did not end there, because of something that neither Jeff nor Sarah could have anticipated when the collaboration began.

Artificial intelligence arrived.

Not the AI of science fiction, but the practical kind: large language models and analytical systems capable of processing the kind of multi-dimensional structural data that the Tholonic framework produced and identifying patterns within it that would take a human analyst weeks to surface. The Tholonic Model, it turned out, was not just compatible with AI analysis. It was, in a structural sense, made for it. The model's output is a five-dimensional map of structural balance across a hierarchical system. That is precisely the kind of data that AI-assisted analysis handles well, because the relationships between dimensions across levels of a hierarchy are exactly the kind of complex multi-variable pattern that machine learning was built to recognize.

The practical consequence was a change in what the model could do. What had previously required months of careful manual analysis could now be done in hours. What had previously been limited to commodity supply chains with rich data could now be extended to any system that changed from one state to another: historical narratives, water purification systems, agricultural value chains, ecosystem health assessments, urban infrastructure. The five dimensions are structural universals. They apply wherever there is a process of transformation with inputs and outputs and constraints.

For Jeff, this meant that the fifty-year project had landed in exactly the moment when it could be used. The formal mathematical structure was in place. The real-world data was in place. The analytical infrastructure to operate at scale was, for the first time, available. A framework built from the oldest patterns in human thought about the nature of reality had found its first genuinely adequate computational instrument at the same moment that the instrument was becoming widely available.

Whether that timing was luck or something else, both of them had enough experience with how ideas develop to know better than to ask too hard.

## Coda: The Shape of What Holds

The Tholonic Model and the Clarity Coalition now share a foundation. The supply chain data that Sarah's network has built over years of careful field work gives the mathematics somewhere real to act. The mathematical framework that Jeff spent decades deriving gives the data a structural depth that outcome-based measurement cannot provide.

What keeps becoming visible, as the work continues, is not just a better method for measuring sustainability. It is a demonstration that there is inherent structure in all natural change. That any process of transformation, whether a commodity moving from extraction to market or an ecosystem recovering from disturbance, follows the same structural logic. And that this logic is not imposed from outside. It emerges from the irreducible relationships between the most basic things: the act of distinction (zero and two), the first three primes (2, 3, 5), and the three roles that any stable system must maintain in proportion.

Fifty years is a long time to carry a question. But some questions are worth carrying that long, because the answer, when it finally arrives, turns out to be about something much larger than the question you thought you were asking.
