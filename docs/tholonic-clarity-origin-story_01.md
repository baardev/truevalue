# The Long Road to the Triangle: How the Tholonic Model Found the Clarity Coalition

*A story of fifty years, a few strange coincidences, and a discovery hiding inside the oldest question anyone ever asked.*

---

## Part One: The Question That Would Not Sit Still

Every intellectual obsession has a point of ignition. For Jeff, it was not a single book or a single class. It was a pattern he kept noticing and could not un-notice.

Across the full sweep of human history, every civilization that built a real intellectual tradition also built a model of reality. Not a map of a territory. Not a calendar or a pantheon. A model: a structured answer to the question of why things exist, how they change, and what holds the world together. The Mesopotamians had it. The Greeks had it. The Taoists had it. The medieval alchemists had it, buried in their cryptic symbol systems and their language of sulfur, salt, and mercury. The Renaissance metaphysicians had it, dressed up in different clothes.

What stopped Jeff was not that these models existed. What stopped him was that they kept resembling each other.

This was not immediately obvious. A Sumerian cosmology and a Taoist philosophical text look nothing alike on the surface. The Greeks wrote in the language of geometry and proportion. The alchemists wrote in deliberate riddles. The Neoplatonists wrote in dense metaphysical abstraction. But once you stepped back far enough, once you stopped reading the language and started looking at the shape of the argument, something persistent appeared. These models did not agree on the content of reality. They agreed on its structure.

There was always a generative source. There was always a process of differentiation, of that source expressing itself into distinct principles or forces or phases. And there was always a third thing: the interaction between those differentiated elements, the negotiation, the return, the reconciliation that produced something stable.

Three. Always three.

Jeff was a young man when this pattern first started nagging at him. He was the kind of person who reads sideways through a library, pulling Spinoza off the shelf because he had been reading Newton, pulling Leibniz because he had been reading Spinoza, pulling the Tao Te Ching because the binary logic of Leibniz suddenly looked, from a certain angle, like something very old dressed in modern notation. He had no institutional home for this investigation. It was not a dissertation. It was not a research program. It was just the question that would not sit still, following him through decades of reading and thinking and setting things aside and picking them up again.

He was able to establish one thing with growing confidence: the pattern was not superficial. In every model of reality that had a genuine body of rigorous intellectual work behind it, regardless of the cultural or temporal context, the structure reduced to the same fundamental triadic relationship. It was not just a philosophical convenience. It appeared in the hard sciences as well. He could not yet prove it mathematically, but he could see it everywhere, and that certainty accumulated slowly, the way geological evidence accumulates, one stratum at a time.

The mathematics remained elusive. He could see the pattern. He could trace it. He could demonstrate it comparatively across traditions. But the formal mathematical language that would make it rigorous, the language that would let him say not just "this is everywhere" but "here is why," kept slipping away.

He carried the question with him into his professional life.

## Part Two: The Architect Meets the Web

By the mid-1990s Jeff was working as an IT architect and researcher, and he had landed at IBM during one of the most fertile and peculiar moments in the history of computing. The internet had existed for years, but the World Wide Web was new. The infrastructure was being built in real time, and the people building it were asking not just engineering questions but genuinely philosophical ones: what, exactly, is information? How does it relate to meaning? Can a machine understand context, or only syntax?

Tim Berners-Lee, the architect of the web itself, was already thinking past the document web toward something he called the semantic web. The idea was elegant and strange. Instead of pages linked to other pages, you would have statements linked to other statements. Information would be encoded not just in text but in structured assertions about the relationships between things. A web that did not just store facts but represented meaning.

The basic unit of this new structure was the triplet: subject, predicate, object. "Gold is a metal." "Mining produces ore." "Refining transforms ore into bullion." Three elements, irreducible. You could not split a triplet into something smaller and still have a meaningful unit of information.

Jeff was working at the edge of this development, thinking through the architectural implications of a web built from triplets rather than documents. And somewhere in that work, something clicked.

The triplet was not just a data structure. It was the same shape as the pattern he had been tracing through fifty years of philosophical and scientific models. The three roles of the triplet, the thing making the claim, the relationship being asserted, the thing being described, mapped onto the triadic structure he had found in everything from Taoist philosophy to Newtonian mechanics to Spinoza's attributes of substance. The semantic web was not just a technology. It was an instantiation of a very old idea in a new medium.

This was not yet a complete theory. It was a recognition. A door opening. But it meant that the abstract pattern Jeff had been tracking was not just a feature of human thought about reality. It was a feature of the structure of information itself.

He filed this away and kept working.

---

## Part Three: The Long Derivation

Years passed. Jeff returned to the mathematics with new tools and a new frame. If the triplet was the irreducible unit, and if the triplet's three roles mapped onto the fundamental structure he had been tracing, then the mathematics should be derivable from the most irreducible mathematical objects he could identify. Not invented. Derived.

He started with prime numbers: not all prime numbers, but the first three. Two, three, and five. These are the primes that cannot be reduced to any product of smaller numbers, and in the set of all primes they hold a unique position because they are also the first instances of the only three elemental numerical identities: duality, triplicity, and the first compound structure. Assign each to a role in the triplet framework. Apply the recursive structure, the same structure that appears in every holarchical system, where each level contains the pattern of the whole and is contained by the next level up. See what emerges.

What emerged was not what Jeff expected, though in retrospect he would say it was exactly what had to emerge.

The five fundamental mathematical constants appeared.

Not approximately. Not suggestively. Precisely, as derivable outputs of the recursive triadic structure applied to the first three primes. Pi. Phi, the golden ratio. The square root of two. The natural logarithm of two. Euler's number, e. These are not arbitrary choices. They are the constants that appear throughout physics, geometry, and information theory as the irreducible scaffolding of quantitative reality. They turn up in the orbits of planets and the spirals of shells and the decay of radioactive material and the growth of populations. They had always been treated as discovered features of the universe, beautiful and perhaps meaningful but not explained. What Jeff found was that they were not separate discoveries but a single structure, all five of them emerging together from the same recursive triadic logic.

This was the moment the model got its name.

The structure was holarchical: each level was a whole unto itself and simultaneously a part of the next level. The geometry was tetrahedral: four vertices, each triple of vertices forming a triangle, and the triangles encoding the three-way relationships at every level. And the structure was recursive, repeating itself through every scale. Tholonia: from "holon," the Koestlerian term for a thing that is simultaneously a whole and a part, with the tetrahedral geometry folded into the name. A tholon was one unit of this structure. The model that described how tholons nested and interacted and produced stable systems became the Tholonic Model.

The fifty-year question had an answer. It was mathematical. It was derivable. And it was the same shape as the oldest question anyone ever asked about why things exist and how they change.

## Part Four: The Meeting That Should Not Have Happened

Here the story takes a turn that Jeff himself, after fifty years of tracking a pattern through the full history of human thought, might have recognized as structurally inevitable. The moment of formalization was the moment of connection.

Sarah Jones had founded the Clarity Coalition out of a different kind of frustration, one more immediate and practical but equally deep in its way. She had spent years working at the intersection of sustainability research and real-world commodity systems, and what she had found was a consistent gap between the language that sustainability frameworks used and the actual structure of the systems they were trying to describe. The frameworks were built around outcomes: carbon emissions, biodiversity metrics, human welfare indicators. These mattered enormously. But they floated above the physical reality of supply chains, which were the actual mechanism through which extraction, transformation, distribution, and consumption occurred. You could not change an outcome without understanding the chain that produced it, and most of the frameworks she encountered were not built to see the chain.

She had built the Clarity Coalition around the conviction that this gap could be closed, that real-world supply chain data, collected rigorously and structured well, could become the foundation for sustainability analysis that actually reached the root of the problem rather than describing its surface.

Jeff and Sarah met the way consequential meetings often happen: obliquely, through a connection neither of them had planned, at an event neither of them had expected to find useful. The conversation started where most professional conversations start, with an explanation of what each person was working on. By the time that explanation had run its course, they had both noticed something.

Sarah's supply chain data was structured exactly the way the Tholonic framework described structure. The chains she was documenting had phases. Each phase had inputs and outputs. Each phase was constrained by specific limiting factors and defined by specific outputs it produced and passed forward. The relationship between what a phase required and what it produced was precisely the relationship the Tholonic Model encoded as the tension between Definition and Contribution, the two poles whose balance determined whether a system was coherent or under stress.

And the real-world data gave the mathematics somewhere to land.

This is the part of the story that is hard to convey without sounding improbable, because the fit was not approximate. Jeff had been developing a mathematical framework for how systems sustain themselves, and Sarah had been collecting data from real systems trying to sustain themselves, and the structure of the one was the structure of the other. The triplets that had opened a door in the IBM years were now appearing as the natural unit of supply chain analysis. The five constants that had emerged from the recursive mathematics were now the five dimensions along which the health of any chain could be measured. The recursive holarchical structure was the supply chain itself, with its phases nested inside the system, and sub-phases nested inside the phases, all the way down to the specific constraint or output that determined whether a given node was functioning or failing.

Neither of them had been looking for the other. But both of them had been, for years, working toward a point where this conversation was the only natural thing to have next.

## Part Five: What Was Discovered

The mathematical result that came out of this collaboration was cleaner and stranger than either of them had expected.

The five constants, pi, phi, the square root of two, the natural logarithm of two, and Euler's number, were not just abstract outputs of the recursive derivation. Each one measured a specific structural dimension of any system. Pi measured operational balance: the ratio that had to hold across the chain as a whole for the system to be coherent. Phi, the golden ratio, measured proportional value distribution: whether the value being created at each phase was being distributed in the proportions that allowed the whole chain to function. The square root of two measured structural overhead: the degree to which constraint was absorbing energy without producing output. The natural logarithm of two measured transformation efficiency: how well the system was converting inputs into outputs across the time lags and capital gaps that every real supply chain contained. And Euler's number measured financial abstraction: how tightly the funding instruments connected to the real operational metrics rather than floating above them.

When you measured a supply chain along all five dimensions and benchmarked it against these constants, you got something that had not existed before: a phase-resolved health map that told you not just that a system was under stress, but precisely where the stress was originating and which structural dimension was responsible for it.

This was not a sustainability scorecard. Those already existed in abundance, and Sarah knew their limitations. This was a diagnostic instrument. It could locate failure before failure became visible in the output. It could distinguish between a system that was struggling because of a structural constraint and one that was struggling because of an efficiency problem, because those two problems look identical in the output numbers but require entirely different interventions.

For Sarah's work, this changed the conversation. Supply chains had always been analyzable in terms of their flows. Now they were analyzable in terms of their structure, and the structure could be compared across chains, across sectors, across scales, using a common mathematical language that did not depend on the specific commodity or the specific geography.

For Jeff, it was the completion of something that had been accumulating for fifty years. The patterns he had traced through Sumerian cosmology and Greek geometry and Taoist philosophy and Leibnizian logic and the architecture of the semantic web had not been leading nowhere. They had been leading here: to a model that was derivable, measurable, applicable to real systems, and grounded in the same mathematical constants that appear at the foundation of physics. The structure he had been tracking through the history of human thought was not a human invention. It was a human discovery. The triangle had always been there. It had taken a long time, and one very useful coincidence, to find the right language to describe it.

## Coda: The Shape of What Comes Next

The Tholonic Model and the Clarity Coalition are now working from the same foundation. The supply chain data that Sarah's network has built over years of careful field work provides the real-world substrate that gives the mathematics traction. The mathematical framework that Jeff spent decades deriving provides the structural analysis that gives the data depth.

What is becoming visible, as this work continues, is not just a better way to measure sustainability in supply chains. It is a demonstration that there is inherent structure in all natural change, that any process of transformation, whether it is a commodity moving from extraction to market or a civilization moving from one form to the next, follows the same structural logic. And that this logic is not imposed from outside. It emerges from the irreducible relationships between the most basic things: the first primes, the simplest recursive operations, the three roles that every stable system must maintain in proportion.

Fifty years is a long time to carry a question. But some questions are worth carrying that long, because the answer, when it finally arrives, turns out to be about something much larger than the question you thought you were asking.
