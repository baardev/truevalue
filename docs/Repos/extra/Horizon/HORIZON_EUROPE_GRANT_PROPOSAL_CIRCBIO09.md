## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

**TrueVal-Chains** is a 54-month, €6 million Research and Innovation Action (RIA) submitted under Horizon Europe Call HORIZON-CL6-2026-01-CIRCBIO-09: *Balancing food security, bioeconomy, climate and biodiversity objectives to unlock sustainable value chains*.

### The Problem

The EU's bioeconomy agenda — backed by the CRCF Regulation (2025), the Nature Credits Roadmap (2025), and the revised CAP — has created a regulatory architecture that demands one thing above all else: **reliable, supply-chain-resolved quantitative evidence** on how agricultural value, environmental performance, and food security interact across the farm-to-market chain. That evidence does not yet exist at the resolution policymakers and markets need. Without it, carbon farming certification risks greenwashing, nature credits cannot be priced credibly, farmers remain price-takers unable to monetise their stewardship, and land-use conflict assessments remain speculative.

### The Solution

This project develops, validates and deploys the **True Value Framework (TVF)** — a quantitative, phase-resolved supply chain intelligence methodology built to produce exactly this evidence. TVF maps each commodity supply chain into discrete, measurable phases, each defined by a physical state, a transformation, a measurable output, and a transparency classification. Across each phase, the framework accounts simultaneously for physical flows, economic value distribution, Scope 1/2/3 GHG emissions, natural capital (biodiversity, water, soil, carbon sequestration), and social equity — generating the integrated accounts that the CRCF, Nature Credits, and EU EAA frameworks require but currently lack.

### Three Commodity Systems — One Comparative Framework

The project applies TVF to three strategically chosen commodity systems that together span the full range of governance environments, supply chain architectures, and policy challenges relevant to the call:

**1. Shea — West Africa to EU** (anchor commodity)  
Mapping the full supply chain from parkland agroforestry in Senegal, Burkina Faso, Mali and Ghana through to EU cosmetics and chocolate manufacturers. Shea is the most direct link between the Great Green Wall (GGW) — the AU/EU's flagship $33 billion Sahel restoration programme, currently only ~2% achieved — and EU corporate Scope 3 obligations under CSRD. The project delivers the first phase-resolved Scope 3 GHG emission factors for shea, unlocking carbon and nature credit revenue for 4–6 million women cooperative members along the GGW corridor.

**2. Olive Oil — Spain and Italy** (EU cooperative anchor)  
Spain is the world's largest olive oil producer; cooperatives account for ~65% of production. Italy is the critical bulk trade destination — the Spain→Italy value flow is a textbook case of value appropriation asymmetry. The project delivers a cooperative business model innovation assessment, olive mill waste circular bioeconomy analysis, natural capital accounts for Andalusian olive systems, and policy recommendations benchmarked against the OECD's existing Spain policy recommendations. The ROBIN Horizon Europe project (Andalusia governance) is identified as a direct complementarity partner.

**3. Diversification Commodities — Argentine Soya/Beef and Spanish Cherries**  
Tests TVF scalability across a high-deforestation-risk Global South commodity (Argentine soya/beef, directly relevant to the EU Deforestation Regulation) and a domestically bounded EU horticultural system (Spanish cherries). Together these generate the cross-governance comparative dataset needed to make the project's policy recommendations universally applicable, not case-specific.

### Methodology

TVF is grounded in the **Negotiation–Definition–Contribution (N-D-C) triadic analytical structure** — a formally validated framework that models each supply chain phase as a system of constraints (D), outputs (C), and emergent stable state (N). Sustainability is measured as the D/C balance: imbalance reveals structural fragility, value leakage, or environmental risk. The framework has been developed and validated on the gold supply chain (Phases 0–7) and is now extended to agricultural commodity systems. TVF is directly compatible with SEEA natural capital accounting, IPBES biodiversity valuation, the GHG Protocol Scope 3 standard, and the EU Economic Accounts for Agriculture (EAA) framework.

## 1. Strategic Alignment and Rationale

### 1.1 The Problem This Proposal Addresses

The EU's bioeconomy and nature market strategies — including the Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming (CRCF) Regulation (EU 2025/2358), the Nature Credits Roadmap, and the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) — require a fundamental transformation of how agricultural value is measured, distributed, and governed. Yet a critical gap persists: **the methods and tools for quantifying the full, phase-resolved True Value of agricultural supply chains — including their environmental, social and climate costs and benefits — are not yet operational at the farm-to-market level.**

Without this analytical infrastructure, policymakers cannot credibly balance bioeconomy incentives against food security safeguards. Farmers remain price-takers, unable to demonstrate the full value of their environmental stewardship. Markets for carbon credits, nature credits, and biodiversity offsets lack the supply chain grounding needed to prevent greenwashing, perverse incentives, and land-use conflicts.

### 1.2 The Opportunity

This proposal develops and validates the **True Value Framework (TVF)** — a quantitative, phase-resolved supply chain intelligence methodology — across three strategically chosen commodity systems:

1. **Shea** (West Africa: Senegal, Burkina Faso, Mali, Ghana) — connecting the Great Green Wall (GGW) initiative and African women's cooperatives to EU cosmetics and food manufacturers
2. **Olive Oil** (Spain, with Italy as the critical bulk-trade market) — the world's largest cooperative-based agricultural commodity, anchored in proven bioeconomy governance models
3. **Diversification commodities** (Argentine soya/beef, Spanish cherries, other agri-food products) — to test framework scalability and cross-regional land-use conflict analysis

This selection is not arbitrary. These three systems together cover: (a) a North-South supply chain with deep Scope 3 GHG and biodiversity implications (Shea/GGW); (b) a mature EU cooperative sector with documented natural capital governance challenges (Olive Oil/Spain); and (c) a Global South commodity with high land-use conflict risk and AfCFTA relevance (Argentina/soya). Together they generate the comparative dataset required to produce generalizable, policy-applicable findings.

### 1.3 Why Now

- The EU Nature Credits Roadmap (2025) requires certification methodologies that do not yet exist at supply chain resolution
- The CRCF Regulation (adopted November 2025) demands monitoring, reporting and verification tools for carbon farming — tools this project will develop and test
- The EU Vision for Agriculture and Food explicitly calls for "fair income for farmers" and "resilient supply chains"
- The GGW received $14 billion in pledges in 2021, yet only ~2% of restoration targets have been met; this project provides the value chain intelligence to unlock that capital
- The deadline (17 September 2026) aligns with growing institutional urgency to operationalize these frameworks before the 2027 CAP review cycle

## 3. Commodities and Geographic Focus

### 3.1 Shea — West Africa to EU (Anchor Commodity)

**Why Shea:**  
Shea is simultaneously one of the most critical bioeconomy commodities linking the Great Green Wall (GGW) to EU markets and one of the most structurally opaque supply chains in global trade. An estimated 4–6 million women in West Africa depend on shea kernel collection and processing for their primary income. EU imports of shea butter feed the cosmetics industry (notably Unilever, L'Oréal, The Body Shop supply chains) and chocolate and confectionery manufacturers (as a cocoa butter substitute) — yet Scope 3 emissions and biodiversity impacts along this chain remain unmapped at the resolution needed for credible certification.

**Scope and Geography:**
- Senegal, Burkina Faso, Mali, Ghana (production zones along the GGW corridor)
- Processing and export hubs: Bobo-Dioulasso (BF), Dakar (SN), Accra (GH)
- EU import/refining: France, Germany, Netherlands, Italy
- End markets: EU cosmetics and chocolate manufacturers; retail sustainability commitments

**True Value Framework Application:**
- Phase-resolved supply chain mapping (collection → kernel processing → butter production → export → refining → manufacturing → retail)
- Full Scope 3 GHG accounting per phase
- Biodiversity and ecosystem services valuation (parkland agroforestry, carbon sequestration, soil health)
- Women's cooperative income diversification modelling (beyond food/feed to cosmetics, energy, organic fertiliser, carbon credits)
- Land-use conflict assessment: shea parkland vs agricultural intensification vs mining concessions

**Connection to Great Green Wall:**
The GGW represents the world's largest ecosystem restoration programme ($33 billion estimated total cost; $14 billion pledged by 2021). Shea agroforestry is a cornerstone of GGW land-use strategy in Senegal and Burkina Faso. This project directly addresses the documented gap: only ~2% of GGW national restoration targets achieved, partly due to absence of functioning value chain infrastructure linking restoration activities to market incentives. TVF supply chain analytics will provide the evidentiary foundation to unlock GGW-aligned carbon and nature credit mechanisms.

**Key Data Partners:**
- National Union of Shea Producers (Burkina Faso) — 5,000 women's cooperatives
- Nitidæ (France) — SHEA GETS GREENER project alumni
- CIFOR-ICRAF — existing research on shea market integration
- Impact Agri (contact: Jon White) — agribusiness supply chain analytics

### 3.2 Olive Oil — Spain (and Italy as Bulk Trade Market)

**Why Olive Oil:**  
Spain is the world's largest olive oil producer, with cooperatives accounting for approximately 60–70% of total production. The Andalusian olive sector is among the most intensively studied in EU bioeconomy governance research, and Spain has received specific OECD policy recommendations on cooperative-sector reform and natural capital integration. Italy is the critical bulk trade destination for Spanish olive oil — processing, re-labelling and distributing Spanish oil under Italian brands to global markets — creating a well-documented value appropriation asymmetry that the TVF methodology is directly designed to analyse.

**Scope and Geography:**
- Spain: Andalusia (Jaén, Córdoba, Granada), Castilla-La Mancha — cooperative sector focus
- Italy: Processing, import, and premium market re-distribution (bulk Spanish → branded Italian)
- Argentina: Olive oil production expansion (Mendoza, San Juan, La Rioja) — emerging Global South competitor

**True Value Framework Application:**
- Cooperative vs. non-cooperative value capture analysis
- PDO (Protected Designation of Origin) and bioeconomy premium quantification
- Olive mill waste (omurgha/pomace) as circular bioeconomy biomass — biorefineries, bioenergy, soil amendments
- Land-use conflict analysis: water stress (olive is water-intensive in an increasingly arid Andalusia), olive monoculture vs. biodiversity corridors
- Comparison of governance models: Spanish coops vs. Argentine private-sector supply chains
- Benchmarking against OECD policy recommendations for Spain (cooperative sector reform, natural capital integration)

**Key Data Partners:**

## 4. Methodology — The True Value Framework (TVF)

### 4.1 Framework Overview

The **True Value Framework (TVF)** is a quantitative, phase-resolved supply chain intelligence methodology that models agricultural supply chains as structured physical and value systems, measuring at each phase:

- **Physical flows**: mass, volume, transformation, custody transfer
- **Economic flows**: costs, revenues, margins, value appropriation by actor type
- **Environmental accounts**: GHG emissions (Scope 1, 2, 3), water, land, biodiversity impact
- **Social accounts**: income distribution, labour conditions, gender equity, community impact
- **Governance structure**: transparency, opacity classification, regulatory compliance

The methodological architecture follows the **Negotiation–Definition–Contribution (N-D-C) triadic framework** (Tholonic model), which structures each supply chain phase as a triadic system where:
- **N (Negotiation)** = the stable, coherent operational state at that phase
- **D (Definition)** = constraints, requirements and boundaries governing what the phase *is*
- **C (Contribution)** = outputs, flows and connections governing what the phase *does*

Sustainability of any phase is measured by the D/C balance: when constraints and contributions are in equilibrium, the phase is most energy-efficient and resilient. Imbalance signals structural fragility, value leakage, or environmental risk.

This framework has been developed and validated on the gold supply chain (Phase 0–7, mineral origin to exchange-registered bullion) and is now being extended to agricultural commodity systems.

### 4.2 Phase Resolution

Each commodity supply chain is mapped to discrete phases, each defined by:
- A physical state of the commodity
- A transformation or custody change
- A measurable output with units
- An assigned transparency classification (High / Medium / Low / Opaque)

**Example phase structure for Shea:**

| Phase | Description | Key Physical State | Custody |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|---------|
| 0 | Parkland ecological system | Shea tree, standing biomass | Community / commons |
| 1 | Kernel collection | Dried kernel | Women's cooperative / collector |
| 2 | Primary processing | Shea butter (artisanal) | Cooperative / aggregator |
| 3 | Export preparation | Cleaned kernel or crude butter | Aggregator / exporter |
| 4 | International transit | Kernel or butter in bulk | Exporter / shipping |
| 5 | Industrial refining | Refined shea olein/stearin | Refiner (EU) |
| 6 | Manufacturing integration | Ingredient in product | Manufacturer |
| 7 | Retail and end consumer | Final product | Retailer |

### 4.3 Natural Capital Accounting Integration

The project will develop and test natural capital accounting methods aligned with:
- **SEEA (System of Environmental-Economic Accounting)** — the UN framework for ecosystem accounts
- **EU Economic Accounts for Agriculture (EAA)** framework — direct call requirement
- **IPBES biodiversity valuation protocols**
- **CRCF Regulation certification methodology requirements** — the project will contribute input to Delegated Acts expected in 2026

For each commodity and phase, a **Natural Capital Account** will be constructed containing:
- Provisioning ecosystem services (food, fibre, fuel)
- Regulating ecosystem services (carbon sequestration, water regulation, pollination)
- Cultural ecosystem services (livelihood, heritage, gender empowerment)
- Biodiversity metrics (species richness, habitat quality, connectivity)

### 4.4 Scope 3 GHG Accounting

Full Scope 3 GHG emission accounting will be conducted for the shea supply chain as the primary pilot (with olive oil as secondary). This addresses a critical gap: cosmetics and food companies with Scope 3 disclosure obligations under CSRD (Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive) lack supply chain-resolved emission factors for shea butter. The project will generate:
- Per-phase GHG emission factors (kg CO₂e per kg commodity)
- Attribution of emissions by supply chain actor type
- Uncertainty ranges and transparency classification
- Integration pathway into existing LCA databases (ecoinvent, etc.)

### 4.5 Biomass Threshold Modelling

A core methodological innovation is the development of **biomass supply-demand threshold models** — quantitative assessments of the market price levels at which bioeconomy incentives (carbon credits, nature credits, bioenergy contracts) begin to divert agricultural biomass from food/feed to non-food uses, triggering food security risks. These threshold models will be calibrated separately for each commodity system and validated against historical market data.

### 4.6 Land-Use Conflict Assessment

The project will apply a **spatial land-use conflict index** to each commodity system, assessing:
- Direct competition between food production, bioeconomy crops, carbon forestry, and nature restoration
- Leakage effects (displacement of food production to non-mapped zones)
- Mining and extractive industry conflicts (particularly relevant for Sahelian shea zones)
- Political economy of land tenure and conflict resolution

## 12. Multi-Actor Approach Statement

The call requires proposals to **implement the multi-actor approach** with a consortium based on a **balanced mix of actors** including farmers, researchers, farm accountants, and businesses.

This project fulfils this requirement through two structural mechanisms:

**1. Consortium composition:** The consortium includes research institutions, a cooperative federation (farmers and farm accountants), private sector supply chain analytics, technology providers, and communication specialists. Each WP Lead represents a distinct actor type.

**2. Multi-Actor Panel (MAP):** A formally constituted advisory and co-creation body with defined roles, meeting schedule, and budget allocation. MAP members include primary producers (shea cooperatives, olive cooperative representatives), farm accountants, EU and African policymakers, and downstream market operators. MAP activities are integrated into WP1 (co-design of framework), WP5 (co-design of policy recommendations), and WP7 (validation of communications).

**Genuine co-creation, not consultation:** Following Horizon Europe MAA guidance, the MAP is not a post-hoc consultation mechanism. MAP members will co-design research instruments (WP1), validate data collection approaches (WP2, WP3), co-author policy recommendations (WP5), and participate in dissemination events (WP7). Budget is allocated proportionally to ensure genuine participation.

## SUPPORTING CONTEXT: Why This Call Is a Strategic Fit

### The EU Has Built the Regulatory Framework — Now Needs the Analytical Tools

In 2025–2026, the EU completed the regulatory architecture for its bioeconomy and nature market vision:
- The CRCF Regulation was operationalised (November 2025)
- The Nature Credits Roadmap was published (July 2025)
- The EU Bioeconomy Strategy was revised
- CSRD is now driving corporate Scope 3 disclosure obligations

What the EU lacks is **the supply chain-resolved quantitative methodology** to operationalise these frameworks. Standard LCA tools and national agricultural statistics lack the phase resolution and North-South supply chain coverage required. The True Value Framework directly fills this gap.

### The Multi-Commodity, Multi-Region Design Maximises Policy Relevance

By spanning West Africa (GGW/shea), Mediterranean EU (olive oil/Spain/Italy), and South America (Argentina/soya), this project generates the **comparative evidence base** that single-commodity or single-region projects cannot produce. The EU needs to understand how bioeconomy and nature market incentives interact with food security risks across different governance environments — this project provides exactly that.

### The Cooperative Sector Is the Key Actor

Both in the olive oil sector (Spain) and in the shea sector (West African women's cooperatives), **cooperative structures are simultaneously the most vulnerable to value appropriation asymmetries AND the most capable of scaling sustainable practices** if given the right analytical and policy tools. This project puts cooperatives at the centre of the research design and the multi-actor approach — not as subjects of study but as co-creators of knowledge and co-authors of policy recommendations.

### The GGW Dimension Provides Global Impact and Political Visibility

The Great Green Wall is the AU and EU's flagship collaborative initiative for African land restoration, climate adaptation and rural development. Connecting this project explicitly to GGW objectives — through the shea supply chain analysis and the natural capital accounting for parkland agroforestry — provides political visibility, alignment with EU–Africa partnership commitments, and a pathway to the $33 billion GGW financing challenge.
