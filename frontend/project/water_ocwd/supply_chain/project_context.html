Project Overview

The Orange County Water District (OCWD) Groundwater Replenishment System (GWRS) in Fountain Valley, California, is the world's largest water purification system for indirect potable reuse, producing approximately 130 MGD (~492 ML/day) of advanced purified recycled water (2023/24). Water is treated through microfiltration, reverse osmosis, and UV/H₂O₂ advanced oxidation before injection into the Orange County Groundwater Basin.

OCWD GWRS operates within a fragmented institutional structure: OC San (Orange County Sanitation District) operates primary and secondary wastewater treatment; OCWD operates advanced treatment and groundwater injection; ~30 independent municipal agencies abstract groundwater from the replenished basin. This creates two inter-institutional handoffs and a broken tholonic loop closure.

⚖️ Tholonic model note: The fragmented institutional structure is the key variable in the Tholonic comparison with Singapore NEWater. Two inter-institutional handoffs (OC San→OCWD at Phase 7/8; OCWD→shared commons at Phase 11) produce lower N stability and a USD 0.37/m³ treatment cost premium over NEWater using identical technology. Tholonic loop closure gap: Phase 11 → Phase 0 loop closure passes through a shared groundwater commons controlled by OCWD as basin manager, but pumped by ~30 independent municipal agencies. OCWD cannot control re-abstraction timing, volume, or agency-level consumption. The Phase 11 child-N does NOT map back to Phase 0 parent-N through a single actor — the tholonic recursion is structurally broken. OCWD bears the full cost of producing recycled water (USD 1.25/m³) but subsidises the entire Orange County water economy without proportional value capture.

Tholonic N-D-C Framework Summary

Three distinct actor domains: (1) ~30 municipal agencies (Ph 0–4); (2) OC San (Ph 5–7); (3) OCWD (Ph 8–11). The transition between OC San and OCWD at Phase 7/8 is the largest D-C discontinuity in the chain. This boundary creates B9=true, B10=true in Event 10, contributing boundary_score=5 — the highest in the recycling loop.


Low-transparency Phase 3: Service reservoir phase has opacity_score=1 (low transparency) because fragmentation across ~30 agencies creates no single data source for aggregate storage. This is the only low-transparency phase in the chain and directly results from the institutional fragmentation — not physical inaccessibility. By contrast, NEWater Phase 3 is medium (physical inaccessibility) because PUB publishes aggregate data for its unified storage infrastructure.


TVPCI implication: OCWD's Phase 8 incurs the OC San→OCWD supply agreement cost. The Tholonic model predicts that each inter-institutional boundary adds coordination overhead that is captured in D without proportional C increase. The accumulated D-C discontinuity over two handoffs accounts for the USD 0.37/m3 treatment cost premium observed in the benchmark model.

Bottleneck

Why sqrt2 and ln2 diagnose this failure: D=228, C=228 are equal, yet balance is only 67.9%. This indicates the balance calculation reflects a different data structure (possibly flags rather than the basic D/C formula), but the structural interpretation is clear: recharge ponds face physical constraints that cannot be operationally resolved. The sqrt2 axis identifies fixed geological constraints: percolation rates at Talbert Spreading Grounds are determined by alluvial geology, not by operational choices. D-flags include: spreading basin clogging by fine sediments, seasonal percolation rate variation (30-70 MGD depending on sediment load), and regulatory total dissolved solids limits for percolated water. The ln2 axis identifies the transformation gap: surface-applied recycled water takes 6-12 months to travel through the vadose zone to the aquifer, creating a temporal D-C gap (high input D, delayed output C).
