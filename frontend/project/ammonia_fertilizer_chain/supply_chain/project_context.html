Global Scope and Chain Scale

Global ammonia production reached 183 million metric tonnes (Mt NH3) in 2020, of which approximately 70% is used in agriculture as fertilizer. Urea, ammonium nitrate, and nitric acid together represent about 91% of nitrogen fertilizer use: ammonia itself is rarely applied directly, and is instead converted into these more stable, transportable products. Roughly 99% of ammonia synthesis hydrogen is currently derived from fossil fuels, with natural gas via steam methane reforming dominant and coal gasification used in some regions, especially China.

Producing ammonia requires approximately 7.7 to 10.1 kWh per kilogram of NH3. Ammonia production consumes roughly 1 to 2% of global final energy consumption (about 8.6 exajoules per year), of which around 40% is feedstock (raw material for hydrogen) rather than process energy. Direct GHG emissions from ammonia production are approximately 450 to 500 Mt CO2-equivalent per year, about 1.3% of global GHG emissions. Average direct emissions intensity is 2.6 to 2.9 tCO2 per tonne of NH3 for typical production; the most efficient steam-methane-reforming plants achieve 1.6 to 1.8 tCO2/tNH3, while coal-based ammonia production emits 3.2 to 4.5 tCO2/tNH3, much higher.

The Nitrogen Loss Cascade and the Biogeochemical Flows Boundary

Global nitrogen-use efficiency (NUE), the fraction of applied nitrogen actually taken up by crops, fell from approximately 80% in 1960 to approximately 30% in 2000 as fertilizer application intensified faster than crop uptake improved. Global cropland NUE has partially recovered to approximately 55% by 2020, though this remains far below the 1960 baseline. This means roughly 45% of applied nitrogen is currently lost to the environment (volatilization as ammonia, runoff as nitrate, and emission as nitrous oxide) rather than taken up by crops.

Of the roughly 100 teragrams of nitrogen (Tg N) processed through the Haber-Bosch route for global agriculture in the mid-2000s, only about 17 Tg N was actually consumed by humans in food (crop, dairy, meat products). This illustrates the scale of the loss cascade through the food system: over 80% of Haber-Bosch nitrogen never reaches a human plate.

When use-phase emissions (approximately 660 Mt CO2e per year, mostly nitrous oxide from fertilizer application) and transportation emissions (approximately 30 Mt CO2e per year) are added to direct production emissions (450 to 500 Mt CO2e per year), total nitrogen-fertilizer-linked GHG emissions reach approximately 1,100 to 1,300 Mt CO2e per year.

This nitrogen loss cascade, ammonia volatilization, nitrate leaching to waterways causing eutrophication and algal blooms, and nitrous oxide emission to the atmosphere, is the primary real-world driver of the biogeochemical flows planetary boundary, one of the nine boundaries in the Rockstrom/Steffen/Richardson Planetary Boundaries framework, and is documented as transgressed (Richardson et al. 2023, Science Advances).

Per this repo's Paper 19 (Mapping Planetary Boundaries to N-D-C Structural Roles, Section 3), the nine planetary boundaries divide into two structural classes. D-type boundaries (climate, land-system change, freshwater, ozone, novel entities) are violated by the failure of constraining systems: weak regulatory limits relative to extraction and transformation pressure. C-type boundaries (biosphere integrity, biogeochemical flows, ocean acidification, aerosols) are violated by the failure of output accounting: uncontrolled throughput exceeding ecological reabsorption capacity. Phase 5 of this chain is a textbook C-type transgression: C (330) vastly exceeds D (190), meaning the chain is generating nitrogen output flows that are not bounded by any effective constraining structure at the field-to-environment interface. This is structurally distinct from a D-type failure such as a freshwater abstraction limit or a land-use cap: the problem here is not that a constraint is being exceeded, but that almost no constraint exists at all relative to the volume of output.

How the Five-Model Constants Diagnose Failure

Each of the five coherence axes (pi, phi, sqrt2, ln2, e) measures a different dimension of systemic health. When a phase has a low balance score, the five-model analysis identifies which type of failure is driving it. This determines what category of intervention can actually close the D-C gap, rather than applying generic solutions.

The N-D-C balance formula is: balance = 100 - (|D - C| / max(D, C)) * 100. A failing phase has C significantly greater than D: uncontrolled output and throughput (C) exceeds the system's constraining and limiting capacity (D). Each constant axis explains a different reason why C is elevated or D is suppressed.

Balance 57.6% | D=190, C=330, gap=140 | Primary failure axes: ln2 and e
Bottleneck: Below 61.8%

Why ln2 and e diagnose this failure: The ln2 axis measures value transformation efficiency, and Phase 5 is the direct downstream consequence of a decades-long transformation failure: global cereal nitrogen-use efficiency fell from approximately 80% in 1960 to approximately 30% in 2000, then partially recovered to only approximately 55% by 2020. That means roughly 45% of applied nitrogen (C = 330, the throughput) is never converted into crop output; it is lost as ammonia volatilization, nitrate runoff and leaching, or nitrous oxide emission. The D side of this phase (D = 190) represents the near-absence of any effective constraint at the field-to-environment interface: diffuse, unmonitored, non-point-source pollution by nature, with essentially no regulatory or physical boundary preventing the loss. The e-axis failure (50.0, the weakest axis in the chain) is the second diagnosis: sustainability-linked agricultural finance and carbon markets for nitrous oxide abatement remain nascent and are rarely tied to measurable nitrogen-use-efficiency outcomes at the farm level, so there is no financial mechanism currently pricing this externality. Per this repo's Paper 19, this is a textbook C-type biogeochemical-flows Planetary Boundaries transgression: uncontrolled output flow (C) exceeding ecological reabsorption capacity, unconstrained by any effective D-side limiting mechanism, structurally distinct from a D-type constraint failure such as a freshwater or land-use boundary.

Field Application
Balance 67.7% | D=210, C=310, gap=100 | Primary failure axes: phi and sqrt2
Bottleneck: Below Chain Pi

Why phi and sqrt2 diagnose this failure: The phi axis (58.0, Failure zone at the chain level) measures proportional value distribution: farmers in nitrogen-deficient regions face access and price barriers to fertilizer while other regions over-apply, so value and physical nitrogen are not proportionally distributed to where they generate the most food output per unit of environmental loss. At Phase 4, this manifests as application rates and timing that are largely uncontrolled at the farm level in most of the world, with little real-time constraint (D) matching the volume of nitrogen applied (C = 310). Farm-level application data is fragmented and rarely centrally reported, which is itself a sqrt2-type structural overhead: the absence of measurement infrastructure is a physical and institutional friction that prevents D from rising to match C. This phase is the direct upstream cause of the Phase 5 failure: nitrogen that is over-applied or misapplied at Phase 4 is precisely the nitrogen that becomes loss at Phase 5.

Note on Green and Blue Ammonia: A Phase 1 Intervention, Not a Phase 4/5 Intervention

Green and blue ammonia production (using electrolytic hydrogen from renewable electricity instead of fossil-fuel-derived hydrogen) is frequently proposed as the primary decarbonisation pathway for this chain. It is precise and important to note what it actually fixes: it addresses Phase 1's D-heavy energy-intensity and emissions burden (sqrt2-type structural overhead from capital-intensive, fossil-dependent synthesis infrastructure), not Phase 5's C-heavy nitrogen loss problem. Even a 100% green-ammonia chain would still lose approximately 45% of applied nitrogen to the environment at Phase 4 and Phase 5, because the loss mechanism is agronomic and biogeochemical (crop uptake efficiency, soil chemistry, runoff hydrology), not related to how the ammonia was synthesized. The two problems require entirely separate intervention categories: green/blue ammonia is a Phase 1 climate-boundary (D-type) intervention, while nitrification inhibitors, precision agriculture, 4R stewardship, and buffer strips are Phase 4 and Phase 5 biogeochemical-flows-boundary (C-type) interventions.
