🇳🇱 Cocoa Netherlands — Industrial Processing and Value Hub

The Netherlands is the world's largest single cocoa processing node: Amsterdam grinds approximately 600,000 tonnes per year in a dense cluster of institutional documentation, EU regulation, and world-class infrastructure. This project models 6 phases from port arrival through EU re-export using the same N-D-C framework as Cocoa International, but with data quality H throughout and a composite Phase 3 that integrates the TrueValue shea supply chain at a 5% weight — reflecting the actual composition of EU chocolate fat systems.

Why the Netherlands Data Is More Robust and More Indicative of Supply Chain Reality

Three structural reasons this project outperforms the global chain in data quality and analytical precision

Institutional Documentation Density

Every phase in the Netherlands chain is anchored by a named institutional data source. Port arrivals are documented by the Port of Rotterdam Authority (public annual statistics). Quality inspection is governed by ECA grading certificates and NVWA food safety records. Grinding volumes are published by ICCO by country and confirmed in Barry Callebaut and Cargill annual reports. The Dutch Sustainability Agreement on Cocoa (IDH, covering 75% of the Dutch market) produces annual KPI progress reports. This contrasts sharply with the global chain, where Phase 1 (Fermentation/Drying at West African farm level) has zero institutional documentation and a 76.5% balance driven entirely by opacity.

Regulatory Traceability Anchor

The Netherlands is the primary EU customs clearance point for European cocoa. Under EU Regulation 2023/1115 (EUDR), every tonne of cocoa entering the Netherlands must be accompanied by a verified deforestation-free due diligence statement from 2025-12-30 onward. This regulatory requirement creates a mandatory documentation trail that does not exist anywhere else in the global chain. NVWA cadmium and lead testing under EC 1881/2006 creates a second compulsory measurement layer. These obligations make the Netherlands chain uniquely auditable — the exact quality of data that N-D-C analysis requires to produce meaningful balance scores.

The Shea Integration Is Physically Here

The global cocoa_international project notes that EU chocolate may contain up to 5% shea-derived CBE as a cocoa butter substitute. But the convergence of the cocoa and shea supply chains is not a theoretical abstraction: it physically happens in Wormerveer, Netherlands, at IOI Loders Croklaan's specialty fats facility. Shea butter imported from Burkina Faso and West Africa (the same supply chain modeled in the TrueValue shea project) is fractionated into shea stearin and blended with cocoa butter here. No other node in the global chain can model this convergence with equal precision. Phase 3 of this project uses the shea project's measured N-D-C values (Phase 4 Export: D=210, C=165, balance=64.0%) at a 5% weight to compute a composite score that reflects what EU chocolate fat systems actually contain.

🫒 Shea Butter Integration — Phase 3 Composite (CBE Manufacturing)

Why shea is more relevant to cocoa_netherlands than to cocoa_international

5% Weighted Composite · IOI Loders Croklaan · Wormerveer NL

EU Chocolate Directive 2000/36/EC permits up to 5% of the cocoa butter content in chocolate to be replaced by Cocoa Butter Equivalents (CBEs), of which shea stearin is the most widely used. This is not a marginal adjustment: in the mass-market chocolate segment, the 5% CBE allowance is routinely used for cost and technical reasons (improved snap, shelf-life stability). The substitution must be declared on product labels as "contains vegetable fats in addition to cocoa butter."

The physical process happens at IOI Loders Croklaan in Wormerveer, Netherlands. Shea butter imported from Burkina Faso, Ghana, and Mali — the same origin supply chain modeled in the TrueValue shea project — is fractionated (dry fractionation or solvent extraction) to separate shea stearin from shea olein. The stearin fraction, which has a cocoa-butter-compatible melting profile, is then blended with other permitted CBE fats (illipe, sal, palm mid-fraction) and sold to European chocolate manufacturers.

In cocoa_international, the shea contribution is a 5% footnote at the global Phase 6 (Manufacturing), far from the data-dense core of the analysis. In cocoa_netherlands, the shea input is a documented composite at Phase 3, where the TrueValue shea project's measured export-phase values are incorporated at precise 5% weight. The shea export phase (D=210, C=165, balance=64.0%) reflects real opacity in West African shea trading systems — and that opacity flows into the Netherlands composite, giving a more accurate picture of the integrated chain than either project alone provides.

Primary Bottleneck: Phase 4 — Dutch Manufacturing (85.1% balance, Fair zone)

Unlike the global cocoa chain where the bottleneck is Phase 1 opacity (76.5%, failure zone), the Netherlands' worst phase is Phase 4 (Manufacturing, 85.1%), which is structural rather than opacity-driven. Two coexisting business models create D-C imbalance: Tony's Chocolonely (no CBE, 100% traceable, above-market farm premium targeting USD 2,700/tonne against a USD 1,400 market rate) operates alongside conventional CBE-using manufacturers (Verkade/Mondelez, private label). Retail buyer concentration (Albert Heijn at ~35% grocery share) and packaging complexity (EU PPWR requirements) add D-constraints. The fact that the Netherlands bottleneck is in the Fair zone at 85.1% (vs global failure zone at 76.5%) reflects the qualitative difference between a documentation-rich institutional chain and the opacity-driven global average.
