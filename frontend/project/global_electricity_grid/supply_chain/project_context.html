Global Generation Mix: 2024 Baseline and IEA Pathway

Global electricity generation reached approximately 30,850 TWh in 2024 (IEA Global Energy Review 2025). Coal remained the single largest source for the 50th consecutive year at 35% of generation (approximately 10,587 TWh), followed by natural gas at 22% (approximately 6,796 TWh), hydro at 14% (approximately 4,417 TWh), nuclear at 9% (approximately 2,765 TWh), wind at 8% (approximately 2,497 TWh), solar PV at 7% (approximately 2,130 TWh), and bioenergy and other sources at approximately 5%. For the first time in 2024, renewables plus nuclear together covered 40% of global generation, with renewables alone contributing 32-33%. Generation from solar and wind rose by a record 670 TWh in 2024; gas generation rose 170 TWh and coal rose 90 TWh over the same period.

Regional milestones in 2024: in the EU, solar plus wind generation share surpassed the combined share of coal plus gas for the first time. In the United States, solar plus wind reached 16%, overtaking coal. In China, solar plus wind reached nearly 20%. These milestones confirm rapid generation-side progress (Phase 1), which is precisely why the transmission and balancing bottlenecks at Phase 2 and Phase 3 are the binding constraint on the system as a whole: the power is increasingly available, but the grid to carry and balance it is not keeping pace.

Grid Investment and Financing Landscape

Interconnection and grid bottlenecks are IEA's own documented primary barrier to renewables integration: grid queues, permitting delays, insufficient transmission capacity, and inadequate storage and balancing reserves. This structural claim is cited generically to IEA Electricity Grids and Secure Energy Transitions analysis; no specific queue-length statistic beyond what is given in this project's grounding data is asserted, and any additional granularity introduced below is explicitly marked PROVISIONAL.

Phase Intervention Worksheet

PROVISIONAL: Recommendations are derived analytically from PDI N-D-C data and public sources. Field validation is required before capital commitment. Each intervention is grounded in the specific mathematical axis that identifies the type of failure.

How the Five-Model Constants Diagnose Failure

Each of the five coherence axes (pi, phi, sqrt2, ln2, e) measures a different dimension of systemic health. When a phase has a low balance score, the five-model analysis identifies which type of failure is driving it. This determines what category of intervention can actually close the D-C gap, rather than applying generic solutions.

The N-D-C balance formula is: balance = 100 - (|D - C| / max(D, C)) * 100. A failing phase has D significantly greater than C: the constraint load (D) exceeds the system's contribution capacity (C). Each constant axis explains a different reason why D is elevated or C is suppressed.

Phi-derived zone thresholds: Coherent at or above 80%; Stressed 61.8-80% (phi-inverse floor); Failure 38.2-61.8%; Breakdown below 38.2%. The e axis (58.0) is the structurally weakest axis in this chain, falling into the Failure zone by the strict threshold. All three below-80% axes (sqrt2, ln2, e) converge on the same two bottleneck phases: 2 and 3.

2
Transmission
Balance 74.1% | D=290, C=215, gap=75 | Primary failure axes: sqrt2 (√2) and e
Bottleneck

Why sqrt2 diagnoses this failure: The sqrt2 axis measures structural overhead: physical or institutional friction that raises D (the constraint load) without adding to C (the contribution capacity). In Phase 2, the structural overhead is the interconnection queue itself: projects wait years for grid operators to study and approve connection, a delay documented by IEA Electricity Grids and Secure Energy Transitions analysis as the primary barrier to renewables integration. Cross-border transmission capacity shortfalls compound the queue delay. The sqrt2 chain score of 68.0 confirms this is a structural and institutional problem, not a market problem. More generation capacity (a market solution) cannot close a gap that originates in permitting and queue processing constraints.

Why e also signals this failure: Capacity markets and green bonds exist in principle (e chain score 58.0, Failure zone) but are not structured around specific transmission buildout or queue-clearance KPIs. Financing exists in the abstract; it is not yet operationally linked to the physical queue backlog.

3
Balancing and Storage
Balance 74.5% | D=275, C=205, gap=70 | Primary failure axes: ln2 and e
Bottleneck

Why ln2 diagnoses this failure: The ln2 axis measures value transformation: how effectively a phase converts inputs into outputs of higher value. Phase 3 is an ln2 failure because the strong renewable capacity additions documented in Phase 1 (approximately 700 GW added in 2024, generation from solar and wind up a record 670 TWh) are not being fully converted into firm, balanced, deliverable power. Some of that generation is curtailed rather than delivered, a documented pattern published by CAISO, ERCOT, and National Grid ESO. The ln2 chain score of 75.0 confirms this is a transformation gap, not a generation shortfall: the energy exists, but the balancing and storage infrastructure to convert it into reliably deliverable power lags behind.

Why e also signals this failure: As with Phase 2, financial instruments for storage and flexibility resources (capacity markets, green bonds) exist but are not consistently linked to specific GWh-deployed or curtailment-reduction KPIs, the same e-axis Failure-zone pattern (58.0) that also constrains Phase 2.

If both Phase 2 and Phase 3 interventions succeed, the chain average balance rises from 81.8% to 87.5%, and no phase remains below the 80% Coherent floor (the weakest remaining phase, Phase 0, sits exactly at 80.0%).
