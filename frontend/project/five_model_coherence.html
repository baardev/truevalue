What Each Model Measures — and Why It Matters

Five orthogonal perspectives on the same supply chain. Each reveals a different class of failure that the others cannot see.

π
π Model
Layer 1 — Operational
D=3, C=5 → π (3.14159…)

What it measures

The D-C balance within each individual phase. Is this phase over-constrained or under-integrated? Are the specific operational requirements (D) matched by the available connections and outputs (C)?

Why it matters

This is the most physically grounded layer — it measures operational sustainability. A phase with D >> C is choking; one with C >> D is unstable. The π constant emerges because the Leibniz series (1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − …) embeds the exact same 3 and 5 values as D and C.

What a high score means

  • Phase operates near peak sustainability
  • Constraints matched by integration capacity
  • Minimal energy cost to maintain equilibrium
Failure mode: D-C imbalance at phase level — over-constrained (D >> C) or over-integrated (C >> D). The physical operation is stressed.
φ
φ Model
Layer 2 — Proportional
D=2, C=3 → φ (1.61803…)

What it measures

The ratio of N-values between adjacent phases. Does value grow at the natural golden-ratio rate as it moves through the chain? Are phase-to-phase transitions proportionally harmonious?

Why it matters

A chain can be operationally healthy at every phase (π coherent) while one phase still systematically extracts disproportionate value from its neighbors. The φ model catches exactly this. φ emerges because D+C = 2+3 = 5, the next Fibonacci term — the structural link to the golden ratio's recursive self-similarity.

What a high score means

  • Value flows naturally through phase boundaries
  • No phase captures more than its structural role warrants
  • Chain scales harmoniously — Fibonacci-like growth
Failure mode: Disproportional value capture. One phase's N-value collapses or surges relative to neighbors — the signature of extraction, bottleneck, or monopoly power.
√2
√2 Model
Layer 3 — Structural
D=2, C=2 → √2 (1.41421…)

What it measures

Whether custody transfer phases (logistics, assay, vaulting) preserve structural balance. What enters a transfer phase should exit in equivalent form — the irreducible overhead of crossing a balanced boundary is √2 (~41% above input).

Why it matters

√2 is the diagonal of a unit square — the most physically realizable irrational, constructible with compass and straightedge. Any boundary crossing imposes this overhead naturally. When a transfer phase imposes more, it has become an extraction point masquerading as a pass-through.

What a high score means

  • Transfer phases are symmetric — input equals output in kind
  • Custody handoffs, logistics, and assay phases impose only natural overhead
  • No intermediary rent-extraction at phase boundaries
Failure mode: Transfer asymmetry — a phase that should be a neutral conduit imposes overhead far exceeding √2. The boundary has become a toll gate.
ln(2)
ln(2) Model
Layer 4 — Growth
D=1, C=2 → ln(2) (0.69315…)

What it measures

Whether transformation phases (processing, refining) double value at the natural rate. ln(2) is the doubling constant — how many natural steps it takes for a continuously compounding process to double. Its series oscillates toward equilibrium: 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …

Why it matters

Processing phases are the natural growth conduits of a supply chain — raw material enters and a transformed, more valuable product exits. When artificial D-institutional constraints are imposed on these phases, they suppress natural doubling and convert a growth engine into a rent-extraction bottleneck.

What a high score means

  • Transformation phases multiply value at the natural rate
  • Minimal artificial constraints imposed on growth conduits
  • Processing phases function as intended — value engines, not toll gates
Failure mode: Conduit capture — artificial constraints (firewood cost, lack of buyer commitment, certification barriers) imposed on phases that should double value naturally. Each constraint damps the oscillatory convergence.
e
e Model
Layer 5 — Abstract
D=1, C=1 → e (2.71828…)

What it measures

Whether abstract and financial claims on the supply chain remain coupled to physical reality. Paper gold, carbon credits, sustainability certifications, and financial derivatives all represent abstract claims. The e model measures the ratio of physical holdings to abstract claims — the coupling coefficient.

Why it matters

e is the base of natural logarithm — the constant of continuous compounding. The abstract layer compounds independently of physical constraints. When it decouples (paper claims grow faster than physical holdings), the abstract system has escaped physical reality. This is the most dangerous failure mode: the financial architecture appears healthy while the physical foundation it claims to represent is failing.

What a high score means

  • Paper claims backed by physical holdings in correct proportion
  • Financial instruments remain tethered to supply chain reality
  • Abstract compounding and physical growth move together
Failure mode: Decoupling — rehypothecation, unallocated gold accounts, uncertified carbon credits, and paper claims multiply beyond what the physical chain supports. The abstract system floats free.
